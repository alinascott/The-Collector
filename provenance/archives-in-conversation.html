Archives in Conversation: Getting the Full Picture

Alina Scott · first published 2018-04-30 on Woven into History

The photo below captures Frederic Allen Williams in his studio in 1940. It is one of many incredible, newly digitized, lantern slides available through a digital project called Frederic Allen Williams: A Citizen Artist with a Magic Lantern. Each of the items in this digitized collection is functionally a part of the collection Williams donated to the University of Texas after his death. While educators and students will find that digital archives can be effective independently, collections that have been separated, like the Williams Collection, are most effective and useful in conversation with other archives. Here is an example.

In 1940, along Frederic Allen William's journey to collect items for his "Horse Museum", he posed for a series of photographs in his studio. One can imagine him positioning most of the items in the room to be symmetric and guide the eye across to each and every one of his "unique" pieces from across the "great" American frontier. Two human-size sculptures stand on either side of the photograph. A belt hangs strategically on the right. Williams sits in the middle of the frame, on a red, black, creme, and tan Chief's blanket, with what appears to be "The Arrowmaker", sitting beside him. A pillow is placed next to the sculpture. The rest of the room includes at least 8 other sculptures, a blanket thrown over a table with a vase and bowl placed on it, a hat, and oddly placed leather pants.

The pillow on William's right was likely not intended to be a pillow or a bag which it is labeled as in archival storage. It is a composite of two samplers, one side with the design of a cross and the other with a zig-zag diamond and four crosses seemingly cut in half for the post-sale transformation into a bag/pillow. Woven samplers were much smaller than full blankets, scarves, and rugs. They offered traders/collectors the opportunity to see design previews before commissioning an item. For Williams, however, it was simply a pillow.

The Chief's blanket William's is sitting on (below) is one of several on loan to the Blanton Museum of Art. Only half of the blanket is visible but seeing as all Chief's are symmetric, one would still be able to get a decent idea of what the entire textile looks like by solely examining the photograph. It is third phase transitional style, meaning it was likely created between 1880-1890, and contains Germantown dyes. Art archivists described it as follows:

"Three broad red bands separated by two broad cream bands. The red bands have brown and black parallel stripes separated by red. The natural bands have one central stripe of brown and black adjacent stripes. These bands are broken on the edges and center by concentric diamond and half-diamond patterns, which are filled with parallel yellow and black parallel stripes in the outer frame and cream and black stripes within the central frame."

What is lost in Williams' studio portrait is the legacy of appropriating Diné textiles, the exchange of goods between weavers, traders and collectors, and how such pieces of art were transformed to create an Americanized vision of Diné women and people in the Southwest.

It is imperative students, educators, and researchers alike, understand the impact of extractive industries, governments, and scholars that promise so much yet under deliver. And more so, to think critically about the ways in which the biases of others are written into the archive, and how our research, if not done properly can reproduce those biases and stereotypes. The Frederic Allen Williams collection itself has so many layers– how it came together, the collector's wishes for it upon being donated, how archivists understood and consequently cataloged it, the influence of traders, collectors, and outside forces that shaped style (design/pattern, market value, dyes, and fabric) and then the sociopolitical, economic, and historical contexts that shaped each of the individuals that came in contact with these textiles.
