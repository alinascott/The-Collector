The question at the door

Every visit begins with one question on a black screen: What have you learned about Indigenous communities… and how? The site is built so students keep returning to the second half of that question. What they know about Diné weaving reached them through a collector, a museum, catalogers and photographers. The site asks them to look at that route as closely as at the objects.

Learning objectives Draft

By the end of the activity, students will be able to:

  • Describe how a museum collection is assembled, recorded and separated, using the Williams Collection as a case.
  • Read a catalog record as a historical source with an author, a purpose and gaps.
  • Identify what the archive does not record, beginning with the weavers' names, and explain why that matters.
  • Connect changes in Diné weaving to the political and economic history of the 1860s–1930s.
  • Support a claim about a connection between two items with evidence and a stated level of confidence.

Before class

  • Explore the three archival boxes yourself and choose the items you want students to start with.
  • Read the cultural protocols. Some photographs show Taos Pueblo people and places, a dance, and Williams dressed as Native, Arctic Indigenous and Mexican people. Decide how you will introduce them.
  • Share the teaching note that appears on every page: the collection may contain harmful language and images of inappropriate dress, and should be read in the context of the teaching materials.

A suggested sequence Draft

  1. The question (5 minutes). Open the site together. After the opening question fades, ask students to write a one-sentence answer before they touch anything.
  2. Open a box (15 minutes). In small groups, students open one archival box and choose a single item. They record what the item is, who made it, who recorded it, and what is missing.
  3. Read the record (15 minutes). On the item's page, students compare the catalog card with the image. On textile pages, the Weaver row reads "Not recorded." Ask what other information the record holds instead.
  4. Follow the thread (10 minutes). Students use the Connections list or the Filmstrip to place their item in time and beside an item from the other archive.
  5. Return to the question (10 minutes). Students revise their opening sentence, focusing on the word how.

Discussion prompts

  • Whose names appear in this record? Whose do not?
  • What did the cataloger think was worth writing down? What words did they use?
  • Williams photographed himself in a studio full of Diné and other Indigenous objects. What story was he telling, and to whom?
  • The collection was split between two archives. What can each archive tell you that the other cannot?
  • Some images on this site are flagged for community review. Who should decide what is shown?

Assessment Draft

Exit ticket: "Before today I knew … about Diné weaving. I learned it from … . Today I noticed that the archive …" A rubric and a longer assignment, such as writing a new label for one textile, can go here.

Grade levels and adaptations Draft

High school, undergraduate survey and methods courses. Notes on timing, scaffolds and extensions to follow.
