A Dine´ (Navajo) weaver sits beneath her loom, positioning herself at the heart of a tradition. Day in and day out she works toward a Chief’s blanket which is roughly 5.25 x 4.25 feet. The loom is much larger than that. She has imagined the design already- nine crosses spread evenly along the width and center, lines of varying size alongside them. Bold blues, reds, and greens are stitched and cross stitched. When it is completed, the blanket will represent a long history of weaving in the plains, by Navajo, Pueblo and other indigenous groups.

Weavers developed a series of socioeconomic ties to manufacturers, traders, collectors and other U.S. consumers that exported the practice of weaving beyond the American Southwest and resulted in the commodification of Native textiles. One key example of this is the Chief’s blanket- yes, like the one on Antiques Roadshow. The Chief’s blanket is key in the commodification of Diné textiles. The vivid colors, mostly reds, and blues are some of the Chief’s blanket’s most noticeable and highly valued identifiers. This blog will discuss on microhistory of the bright red dye, and the ways it was transformed to increase what traders felt they could sell it for.

The weaving style of Chief’s blankets is categorized into three phases. The first phase lasted until about 1865 when Navajo blankets typically were made with stylistically simple patterns with mostly brown and blue stripes. The second phase, which occurred between 1850 and 1880, included a larger array of design elements and colors, like rectangles, reds, and greens. The third phase of what is referred to as the classical period occurs between about 1880 and the end of the century. In blankets of this period, bright reds were accompanied by diamonds and geometrical motifs. One of the key features of the Chief’s blanket is the consistency of the red. However, early traders catalogs, magazines and newspapers suggest controversy method manufacturers used to create the bright and unwavering red in the third phase of Navajo rug production.

Turkey Red

Textile and weaving experts suggest the first type of red dye introduced into European weaving originated in Turkey, thus the name “Turkey Red”. According to Barbara Brackman, “in the 1750s the secret of Turkey red dyeing was leaked to the European continent where French and English mills began producing Turkey red plain and print cottons.” This process was so lengthy and complex, the final Turkey Red product was often three times the cost of quicker, industrialized red dyes. This process was so complex, it was highly valued. Such margins provided ample encouragement for European weavers to utilize alternate methods of dying, like the one originating from madder root, or in the case of some American traders, fabricate a connection to Turkey Red with a new, simpler, and cheaper American element.

Bayeta (Balleta)

Europeans soon realized - thanks to indigenous knowledge- a bright red could also be extracted from a cheaper, more accessible set of ingredients. The cochineal beetle- which makes its home on a cactus- can produce vivid bayeta dyes. Bayeta dye-making is believed to have originated in Latin America and Mexico. Once discovered by Europeans, colonists exported the cochineal beetle and its carminic acid to be shipped across the Atlantic, and later, to Germantown, Pennsylvania to be converted and resewn into a variety of colors. Like the madder dye, bayeta was long-lasting and its garments garnered significant value. However, they cost close to nothing to make.

Seeing an opportunity to further market their products, some traders stepped in. Traders' sales catalogs and magazines of the Southwest often included photographs of Navajo textiles and descriptions of blankets and rugs made with Turkey Red! However, we have established that the original method of producing turkey red- the madder root- was only available in Europe, and that could not explain the red in western indigenous textiles long before the arrival of Europeans.

Were traders intentionally deceptive to customers by drawing a connection between bayeta dyes and turkey red? Or, were there cultural and historical factors traders believed to be true, such as a possible connection between the Navajo and the Orient, that could also be marketed? Also, why were traders so quick to establish the quality of bayeta over mechanized dyes, while simultaneously connect it to Turkish products?

They simply had a product to sell.
There seemed to be contention between a supposedly handmade bayeta blanket and another more mechanized dyed cloth made in Germantown. Charles Avery Amsden wrote that dye made in “Germantown [which had] a wooly and soft rather than a silky, hard, and rough feel.” Though the efficiency of mechanization would force bayeta to be replaced by the mechanized the Germantown-made dye at the end of the nineteenth century, according to James, it did not compare in quality to bayeta. Even as it became less efficient to use bayeta, it served business to highlight more expensive and higher quality elements of the weaving process.

Such associations were intentionally drawn by traders to increase the sales value of the textiles. Though the processes differed, many believed this dye, despite its American origins, had if not material, symbolic connections to the Orient. Charles Lummis, in an 1898 column on Navajo rugs says, “This red material is from a fine Turkish woolen cloth called balleta. It used to be imported to Mexico, whence the Navahos [sic.] procured it at first...The Navahos used to ravel this cloth and use the thread for their finest blankets; and it made such blankets as never have been produced elsewhere.”

In the chapter of Navaho Weaving called “Bayeta--1800-1863”, Amsden openly addresses the inaccuracies of Lummis’s column:

The romanticists are fond of telling too that bayeta was a Spanish fabric. Lummis (who was never found tamely plodding in another’s track) rose to yet greater heights and derived if from distant Turkey (1896) for no stated reason.

Amsden goes on to admit much of the earliest bounds of bayeta transported to the southwest were taken there through the Spanish in Mexico, the result of which was “a considerable leaven of European influence came into the native culture of the Southwest and found in varying degrees a permanent place therein.” On one hand, Amsden castigates Lummis for drawing too strong a connection between the Navajo and the Orient, and on the other, he expresses a profound European influence on the Navajo weaving culture. One is left to wonder who Amsden believed influenced European weaving practices.

Nevertheless, this conflation of the two dying methods is a normative quality of nineteenth and early twentieth- century writings about Navajo textiles. The trader’s history of dyes was used to both increase the value of the product, and add character to a seemingly obscure element of the weaving process. By the early 20th century, despite technological developments making mass-produced textiles more inexpensive and accessible, handmade Navajo and Oriental rugs were still in high demand. The narratives expressed by Brackman, Amsden, Lummis, and others reflect the intention of traders and Southwestern specialists to paint a however fictitious correlation between the Orient and Native weavers.

Although technological developments made mass-produced rug and carpet production more inexpensive and accessible, handmade Navajo and Oriental rugs were still in high demand. The bayeta dye sometimes referred to as “Turkey Red” was just one element of the process of weaving commodified and marketed to paint an ever-alluring image of the Great American Desert that connected it to a distant and very different Middle Eastern desert, or as J.B. Moore unabashedly writes, “these Bedouins of the great Southwestern Desert”.
